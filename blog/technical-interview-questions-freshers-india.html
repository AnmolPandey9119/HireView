Technical Interview Questions & Prep Guide for Freshers in India

Updated 10 August 2026 · 7 min read

The technical round is where most freshers lose confidence — not because they don't know the material, but because explaining it out loud, under a bit of pressure, is a different skill from solving it silently on paper. Here's what actually gets asked, what interviewers are checking for, and how to prepare without drowning in every topic at once.

1. Core CS fundamentals

Q: Explain the difference between an array and a linked list.
A: Cover memory allocation (contiguous vs scattered), access time (O(1) vs O(n)), and insertion/deletion cost. Give one real scenario where you'd pick each.
Why it's asked: it's a quick check of whether you understand trade-offs, not just definitions.
Q: What is the time complexity of your solution, and can it be improved?
A: State Big-O confidently, then think out loud about a faster approach even if you don't fully solve it — interviewers often care more about the reasoning path than a perfect answer.
Why it's asked: tests whether you think about efficiency, not just correctness.
Q: Explain OOP concepts with an example from a project you built.
A: Don't just define encapsulation/inheritance/polymorphism — tie each one to a real class or module from your own project so it's clear you've actually used them, not memorized definitions.
Why it's asked: textbook definitions without application are an easy tell that you haven't built anything real.

2. Your projects and resume

Q: Walk me through this project on your resume.
A: Structure it as: problem → your specific contribution → one technical decision you made and why → outcome/result. Avoid team-speak like "we built" when describing your own part.
Why it's asked: this is usually the single most-asked technical question — it reveals whether you actually understand what you built or just followed a tutorial.
Q: Why did you choose this tech stack / library?
A: Give a real reason — familiarity, a specific feature you needed, team constraint — rather than "it's popular." It's fine to admit you'd choose differently now with more experience.
Why it's asked: checks if choices were deliberate or copy-pasted from a tutorial.
Q: What was the hardest bug you had to fix in this project?
A: Pick a real one, explain how you debugged it (logs, breakpoints, isolating the issue) step by step, not just the final fix.
Why it's asked: debugging process reveals more about your actual skill level than the final working code does.

3. Data structures & algorithms (DSA)

Q: Solve a basic array/string problem on the spot (e.g. reverse a string, find duplicates).
A: Talk through your approach before coding — clarify constraints, mention edge cases (empty input, duplicates, single element) out loud, then code. Silence while typing makes interviewers nervous.
Why it's asked: communicating your thought process is often weighted as heavily as getting the right answer.
Q: What data structure would you use for [scenario], and why?
A: Practice matching common scenarios to structures — LRU cache → hashmap + doubly linked list, browser history → stack, task scheduling → queue/priority queue.
Why it's asked: real engineering is choosing the right tool, not just knowing every tool exists.

4. System basics & CS theory

Q: What happens when you type a URL into a browser and hit enter?
A: DNS lookup → TCP/TLS handshake → HTTP request → server processing → response rendering. You don't need deep networking expertise as a fresher — a clear high-level flow is enough.
Why it's asked: a classic check of whether you understand the systems your code runs on top of.
Q: Explain normalization in databases, or the difference between SQL and NoSQL.
A: Keep it practical — normalization reduces data redundancy; SQL suits structured, relational data with strict schemas, NoSQL suits flexible/unstructured data at scale. One example of each you've used helps.
Why it's asked: most fresher roles touch a database eventually — this checks baseline readiness.

A quick note on preparation

You don't need to master every DSA topic or every framework before your interview. Go deep on the 2-3 projects on your resume — you should be able to defend every line of decision-making in them — and keep your fundamentals sharp enough to reason through a new problem out loud, even if you don't land on the perfect answer immediately.
